Basic lens properties

The five things every lens must be told, plus the choice of which classical forms to search. These are always applied — there is no switching them off, and every system you get back will match them.

Desired System Units

Control
Millimeters · Centimeters · Inches
Default
Millimeters
Applies to
every length

The unit for every length you type and every length reported back: focal length, package length, image clearance, spot diameter, and the radii and thicknesses in the output file.

Changing this does not rescale an existing specification — it changes how the numbers in the boxes are read. An entry of 35 means 35 mm or 35 inches depending on what is selected here.

Technical

Written into the output file as its dimension record — DIM MM, CM or IN — so the receiving program interprets the prescription correctly. Wavelengths are the exception: they are always in nanometres, whatever is selected here.

Effective Focal Length

Control
number > 0
Unit
system units
Default
35

How strongly the lens converges light, and with the field angle it sets how big the image is. This is the single number that fixes the overall scale of every design you get back.

Every system in the results will have exactly this focal length — you will see the same value repeated down the column. It is not something the search trades away.

Technical

Held exactly rather than approached. On every evaluation the design is scaled uniformly — all radii and all air spaces multiplied by one factor — which moves the focal length to the target without changing the lens's shape, and the image plane is then refocused. Because focal length scales linearly with a uniform scaling, this is exact, not iterative.

One consequence worth knowing: aberrations scale with the lens, so doubling the focal length at a fixed F/number roughly doubles the spot diameter reported in the results.

Pupil Specification

Control
F/Number · EPD · NA
Unit
ratio · length · ratio
Default
F/Number 2.0

How much light the lens collects — its speed. Pick whichever of the three ways you normally think in, and type the value in the box underneath:

  • F/Number — focal length divided by pupil diameter. Smaller is faster: F/2 collects four times the light of F/4.
  • Entrance Pupil Diameter — the working diameter of the beam entering the lens, in system units.
  • Numerical Aperture — the half-angle of the cone, the usual measure for microscope objectives and fibre work.

They are three descriptions of one thing, so setting any one fixes the other two. Speed is the input that costs you the most: a faster lens needs more elements and more careful correction to reach the same spot size.

Technical

Converted internally as EPD = EFL / (F/#) and NA = 1 / (2 · F/#). The entrance pupil is an object-space quantity: the marginal ray arrives at the first surface at a height of EPD/2. It is not the diameter of the stop — the stop's size follows from wherever the design puts it.

For a microscope objective the value is read as the object-side NA instead, and the output uses an object numerical aperture record rather than an entrance pupil diameter.

Semi-Field Angle

Control
number, 0–90
Unit
degrees
Default
30

How wide a scene the lens has to cover, measured from the axis out to the corner — half the total field of view. Entering 30 asks for a 60° full field.

This is the other half of the image-size calculation: the image height at the corner is the focal length times the tangent of this angle. It is also what makes a design hard — the off-axis aberrations grow quickly with field, so a wide-angle request will push the search toward retrofocus forms and more elements.

Technical

The chief ray is traced at this angle and forced through the centre of the stop. Field aberrations scale with powers of it: coma with the field, astigmatism and field curvature with its square, distortion with its cube. The spot diameter in the results is averaged over three zones — on axis, 70% of the field, and the full corner.

Wavelength Range — Long and Short

Control
two numbers
Unit
nanometres
Default
650 long · 450 short

The band of light the lens has to work over — the red and blue ends of your waveband. 450–650 nm is a normal visible request; a laser system might use a band only a few nanometres wide, and a monochrome sensor something narrower than the default.

This directly controls how hard the search works on colour. A wide band forces glass pairings that cancel chromatic error and usually costs an element; a narrow band frees the design to spend its effort elsewhere.

Technical

Three wavelengths are written to the output — long, short, and the midpoint between them — with the midpoint used as the reference for first-order calculations. Glass dispersion is reconstructed for the band you asked for rather than assumed, so the colour correction genuinely tracks these two numbers.

Entering them the wrong way round is harmless; they are swapped automatically.

Starting lens form

Control
Auto, or one named form
Default
Auto (search all forms)

Which classical lens types the search is allowed to build from. Left on Auto it picks the form that best suits your operating point, then tries the others as well — that is where the variety in the results table comes from. Choose a named form to restrict the whole search to that one lineage, which is what you want when you already know the shape you're after.

Technical

These ranges are comfort zones, not limits — a request outside them still works, it just means that form starts further from home. On Auto, forms are tried in order of how far your operating point sits outside each one's zone.

Selecting Microscope objective switches the whole calculation to a finite conjugate and reveals two more boxes: Working distance, the object-to-first-surface distance that the design is scaled to, and Object field height, how much of the specimen is imaged. Focal length is not held in this mode — the working distance takes its place.

Optional lens properties

Constraints from the rest of the product — the housing, the sensor, the budget. Each has a checkbox: unticked, it is ignored completely and contributes nothing to the search. Ticked, it becomes a target the designs are pushed toward and then measured against, and its column in the results is shaded green where there is comfortable margin and red where the limit is being pressed. A system that cannot meet every goal is still shown, flagged rather than hidden.

Maximum Package Length

Control
checkbox + number
Unit
system units
Default
on · 250

How long the lens is allowed to be overall, front glass to sensor. This is the housing constraint — if the lens has to fit a 60 mm barrel, put 60 here.

Tightening it is one of the most effective ways to change the character of the results: a short limit pushes the search toward telephoto-style forms that fold a long focal length into a short tube.

Technical

Measured from the vertex of the first surface to the image plane, so it includes the image clearance and excludes the object distance.

Minimum Image Clearance

Control
checkbox + number
Unit
system units
Default
on · 22

The smallest acceptable gap between the last glass surface and the image plane. This is the room you need for everything that lives in front of the sensor — a filter, a cover glass, a prism, a shutter, or simply the mechanics of mounting.

Ask for a lot of it and you are effectively asking for a retrofocus design, since pushing the focus far behind the last element is exactly what that form does.

Technical

The final air space of the focused system. The image plane is placed at best focus on every evaluation, so this is the true back working distance, not a nominal figure.

Maximum Distortion

Control
checkbox + number
Unit
percent, ±
Default
on · 1.0

How much straight lines are allowed to bow at the edge of the frame. Entering 1 permits the corner of the image to sit 1% away from where perfect geometry would put it.

A useful sense of scale: measurement and machine-vision optics are usually held below a fraction of a percent, general photography tolerates a few percent, and a wide-angle lens left unconstrained will happily produce much more.

Technical

Third-order distortion at the corner of the field, expressed as a percentage of the ideal image height. It is a pure ratio — independent of focal length — so the number means the same thing whatever scale of lens you are designing.

Chief Ray Angle at Image at Maximum Field

Control
checkbox + target + tolerance
Unit
degrees
Default
off · 0 ± 10

The angle at which light arrives at the corner of the sensor. Digital sensors have micro-lenses over each pixel, and they are built expecting light within a particular cone — feed them a steeper angle and the corners lose brightness and pick up colour shading. Sensor datasheets quote the angle they want; put it in Target and the allowed spread in Tolerance.

A target near zero asks for a telecentric design, where the corner rays arrive square to the sensor. That is demanding, and usually costs both elements and length.

This goal bites harder than any other. Left off, a fast 30° design typically lands somewhere between 25° and 33°; switch it on with a target of 10° and a tolerance of 4° and the same search returns designs at about 14° — right at the edge of the band you allowed.

Technical

Measured as the angle the chief ray makes with the axis in the final space, at the full field. Only the excess beyond target ± tolerance is penalised — anywhere inside the band is treated as equally acceptable.

Minimum / Maximum Number of Elements

Control
two checkboxes + numbers
Unit
count
Default
on · 4 to 8

How complex the lens is allowed to be. Element count is the main driver of what a lens costs to make, so this is the commercial constraint as much as the optical one.

The two ends do different jobs. The maximum keeps the result affordable. The minimum is the more interesting one: raising it tells the search it is allowed to spend glass, and it will use the extra elements to correct harder — so if your results look poor, raising the minimum is often the first thing to try.

A cemented doublet counts as two elements, since it is two pieces of glass with two different refractive indices.

Technical

Unlike the other goals this one filters before it scores: candidates outside the range are discarded before any optimization, so the range decides which structures are ever attempted. That is why widening it changes the character of the results rather than just relabelling them.

OptiForge options

How much work to do, and what to call the results. Neither of these changes the optics.

Number of Desired Systems

Control
number, 1–20
Default
10

How many designs to return in the summary table. They come back ranked, best first, so asking for more does not dilute the top of the list — it just shows you further down it.

Ten is a good working number: enough to see genuinely different approaches to the same specification, few enough to compare by eye.

Technical

The search is not scaled down for a small request — the same candidates are explored either way, and you simply get fewer of the survivors. Asking for 4 therefore takes about as long as asking for 10. Near-identical designs are removed before ranking, so the systems you see are meaningfully different from one another rather than small variations on one answer.

Base File Name

Control
text
Default
Camera

The stem for naming the generated systems. Entering Camera gives you Camera_01 through Camera_10, numbered in rank order — that is the name in the results table, the name of the file you download, and the title recorded inside the file itself.

Worth setting to something descriptive when you are comparing several runs, so the downloads don't collide in your folder.
